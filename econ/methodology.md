# How we handle damage figures

Methodology for the economic damages section of The Long Swell.
Written to be read cold, by someone deciding whether to cite us.

Version 0.2, 2026-07-29. Draft. This page will change; changes are
dated and the reasoning is kept rather than overwritten.

A note on who this page is for, since the site's pages are written for
a general reader. **This one is not, deliberately.** Its audience is
the journalist, researcher or editor checking whether our numbers can
be cited, and that person needs the technical detail rather than a
simplified version of it. The damage pages themselves are written to be
understood in seconds by someone with no background. Both are correct
for their reader, and this page should not be levelled down to match
the other.

---

## The one sentence

**We do not produce loss estimates. We compile other people's, show
who said what and when, and keep the history when they change their
minds.**

Every figure in this section belongs to a named organisation, carries
their issue date, and stays in their own currency and units. If you
find a number here that is ours rather than theirs, that is a bug and
we want to hear about it.

## Why this section works differently from the rest of the site

The rest of The Long Swell measures things. Fire counts hotspots
against the same week in past years. The ENSO tracker follows an index
against its own record. There is an instrument, a series, and a
baseline, and the honest claim is a comparison.

Damages has none of that. **There is no thermometer for money.** What a
disaster cost is not measured, it is estimated, by different
organisations, using different methods, counting different things,
arriving months or years apart, and revising afterwards. A page built
on the conventions of measurement would misrepresent that, so this
section carries a different set of disciplines. They are below.

## The seven categories, and why we never add them up

The single most common error in disaster cost reporting is adding
figures that measure different things. These seven never sum:

| Category | What it counts |
|---|---|
| Insured loss | Claims paid or expected to be paid by insurers |
| Direct economic | Physical damage to assets, insured or not |
| Total economic | Direct damage plus indirect and knock-on effects |
| Output loss | Production or labour foregone, with no asset destroyed |
| Humanitarian appeal | Funding requested, which is not loss incurred |
| Mortality | Deaths and health burden, counted in people |
| Monetised mortality | A price placed on those same deaths |

Two consequences worth stating plainly.

**A humanitarian appeal is not a loss.** It measures what agencies
judge fundable, and it systematically understates need rather than
measuring it.

**Monetised mortality is not independent of the death toll.** When the
2015 Indonesian fires are described as causing about 100,300 excess
deaths (Koplitz et al., 2016) and USD 7.3 billion in health costs
(Kiely et al., 2021), those are one fact stated twice, not two sources
agreeing. Wherever both appear here, the link is shown.

## Insured and economic figures are not equally reliable

This is not a caveat, it is the most useful thing on this page.

In July 2026, Aon and Gallagher Re both published global catastrophe
totals for the first half of the year, seven days apart. Their
**insured** loss figures were USD 47 billion and USD 46 billion, about
2% apart. Their **economic** loss figures were USD 111 billion and USD
142 billion, 28% apart.

That gap is structural. Insured losses are built from claims someone
actually paid, so independent compilers converge on them. Economic
losses are modelled, include uninsured and informal-sector damage, and
depend on assumptions each organisation makes differently. The
uninsured share is precisely the part nobody measures directly.

Two dollar figures side by side are not two attempts at the same
quantity. We mark which is which.

## Estimates move, and the movement is the record

Every figure here carries the date its author issued it, separately
from the date we read it. When an estimator revises, **we add a row; we
never edit the old one.** The history stays visible.

Some estimators publish on a schedule and some do not. PERILS, for
example, commits to an initial estimate six weeks after an event ends,
then at three, six and twelve months, closing at thirty-six. For
Windstorm Goretti in January 2026 that produced EUR 467 million, then
EUR 479 million, then EUR 468 million: a total movement of about 2.5%.
A stable estimate is information, and a page showing only the latest
figure would hide it.

Others move much more. Estimates for the January 2025 Los Angeles
wildfires rose from USD 52 to 57 billion to USD 250 to 275 billion in
roughly four days. Two different things were happening at once there:
the fires were still burning, so the event was growing, and knowledge
of it was growing too. Nothing in the published record separates those.
Where we can tell them apart, we say which is which; where we cannot,
we say that instead.

## Apparent disagreement is often a scope difference

Two figures that look like a contradiction are frequently two answers
to different questions, and the estimators usually say so.

The World Bank put the 2015 Indonesian fires at USD 16.1 billion.
Kiely et al. put them at USD 28 billion. That reads as a 1.7x
disagreement. Decomposed, their estimates of physical damage to land
and forest agree within about 2%, and the whole gap is two valuation
choices: what a tonne of carbon is worth, and whether long-term
mortality is counted at all. The World Bank's own report says that
"adding in regional and global costs mean the actual figure is much
higher."

The same shape appears in the June 2026 UK heatwave, where the
Grantham Research Institute's GBP 1.15 billion and Verdant's GBP 2.36
billion differ mainly because the lower figure counts hours not worked
and the higher one also counts reduced productivity and equipment
failure. Grantham says in its own text that its number is probably an
underestimate for exactly that reason.

Where an estimator does not document its scope, we cannot do this, and
that absence is itself worth knowing.

## When there is no number, we say why

A missing figure is never a small figure. There are three different
reasons a damage estimate may not exist, they look identical on a page,
and they mean opposite things:

- **Below a publication threshold.** PERILS publishes nothing for a
  European windstorm under EUR 300 million in one country. Here silence
  is weak evidence the event was small, because somebody was watching
  and told you where they stop looking.
- **Outside anyone's coverage.** No estimator covers Mediterranean
  wildfire. PERILS excludes wildfire as a European peril and excludes
  Spain, Portugal and Greece as territories on either count. Silence
  here says nothing whatsoever about size.
- **Present but not yet valid.** Some seasonal agricultural indicators
  point the wrong way early in a season, confidently. We treat those as
  absent rather than as weak evidence.
- **Not selected upstream.** Our hazard channels flag events by how
  unusual they are against their own history, which is the right test
  for "how abnormal is this" and the wrong one for "where is the most
  damage". In the week to 28 July 2026, Spain was flagged at 14.1 times
  its normal fire activity on 4,678 satellite detections, while the
  Democratic Republic of the Congo, running an ordinary year at 0.98
  times normal, recorded 75,849. Sixteen times the fire, not flagged,
  correctly. We do not let that selection decide which events get a
  cost page.

This has an uncomfortable consequence we would rather publish than
hide: **the places where a damage figure arrives slowest, or never, are
the places with the least insurance.** Loss-estimation infrastructure
follows insurance penetration, so the events with the worst human
outcomes are frequently the ones with no money attached to them,
permanently.

## Three labels you will see on every item

**Attribution**: ENSO-loaded window / not ENSO-linked / attribution
pending. A loss figure attached to an event is never a claim that El
Nino caused that event.

**Evidence basis**: Measured, Compiled or Combined. Almost everything
in this section is **Compiled**: named sources set side by side with no
new number created by us. Where we join layers into a view no single
source states, the item is labelled **Combined**, and every layer
underneath it is itself Measured or Compiled.

**Authorship**: every figure here is an agency figure. If that ever
changes, the label changes with it, prominently.

## Why we do not show damage rising over time

This is the chart everyone expects from a section like this, and we do
not publish it.

Disaster losses have gone up over the last fifty years. Most of that
rise is not climate. It is that there is far more property, worth far
more money, in places where disasters happen, and that a 1975 dollar
and a 2026 dollar are different units. Any honest trend has to remove
those effects first, and that step is called normalisation.

The problem is that normalisation is a choice, not a calculation.
Adjust by national wealth, by population and housing stock, or by
sector exposure, and you get materially different answers from the same
loss record; the published literature disagrees about which is right.
So a normalised loss trend is a methodological claim about a contested
question, and if we picked one we would be making that claim
ourselves. That is the same line we hold on individual figures.

**So: we publish a loss trend only when a named source has published
the normalised series, and then it is theirs, with their method named.**
We do not normalise a loss series ourselves, and we do not show an
unnormalised one, because an unnormalised loss trend mostly measures
economic growth while looking like it measures climate.

This also means the baseline-drift comparison used elsewhere on this
site, showing how far normal has moved, is carried on **hazard**
measurements rather than on money. Hectares burned in 1975 and hectares
burned in 2026 are the same unit. Euros of damage are not.

We are stating this rather than quietly omitting the chart, because a
missing chart with no explanation is indistinguishable from an
oversight. If we are willing to decline the single most expected
graphic in our own subject, and say why, that is a fair test of whether
the rest of this page means anything.

## What we do not do

- We do not average competing estimates. An average of figures that
  measure different things measures nothing.
- We do not sum across categories, ever.
- We do not convert currencies silently. Figures stay in the units
  their author used, with the currency and, where stated, the currency
  year.
- We do not rank estimators by credibility. We tell you what kind of
  organisation each one is and let you weigh it.
- We do not publish a figure from an estimator that does not document
  its method without a documented estimator alongside it.
- We do not model. We have no in-house damage model and no plans for
  one.
- We do not normalise loss series ourselves, and we do not show
  unnormalised loss trends. See above.

## An open question we would rather show than hide

Readers want a single number, and this section usually gives them a
range instead. Whether The Long Swell should surface one central figure
from a range of named estimates, with the range shown around it, is
under active discussion and is not settled. The argument for is that a
range is less usable than a number and people will take a number from
somewhere regardless. The argument against is that any central figure
would be ours rather than anyone else's, and that it is the part that
gets quoted alone.

When this is decided, the decision and its reasoning will be published
here rather than appearing silently in the data.

## Corrections and archives

Published issues freeze. A revised estimate is a new row, never an edit
to an old one, and a dated page stays as it was published including
anything later found to be wrong. Corrections are made forward, in the
next issue, and are stated as corrections.

## Sponsorship

Some organisations whose figures appear here may also sponsor The Long
Swell. A full disclosure policy, covering how estimators are selected
and what sponsors do not get, will be published on this page before the
first damage ledger issue. It is drafted and not yet adopted.

## Sources and how to check us

Every figure links to its source. The full estimator registry, with
each organisation's categories, revision cadence, licence terms and the
date we last checked it, is published as data at
`econ/data/estimators.json`, and the publication-timing map at
`econ/data/latency_map.json`.

If a figure here is wrong, or if you find one that is ours rather than
a named estimator's, tell us and we will fix it in the next issue and
say that we did.
