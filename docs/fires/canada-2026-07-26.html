Canada is having its third-heaviest fire week since 2012, and today is barely counted yet

Satellites have already detected more than twice the normal amount of fire in Canada in the week to July 26. Only 2014 and the record 2023 season have produced a heavier equivalent week, and 2023 is within reach once today is fully observed.

Today is only fractionally observed. Canada's daytime satellite passes happen between about 17:00 and 23:00 UTC and have not occurred yet, so today's 281 detections come from overnight orbits alone, against 4,500 to 15,000 on recent complete days. The week total shown is a floor and will rise materially tonight; it needs 8,397 more to pass 2023.

Canada's fire load is dominated by remote boreal burning, so its year-to-year swings are enormous: this same week produced 859 detections in 2020 and 92,322 in 2014. The Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre reported 3.37 million hectares burned nationally by July 24, about 18 percent above the ten-year average, with the Northwest Territories at roughly 3.9 times its own average and British Columbia below average. Activity in our satellite series peaked on Thursday and dropped sharply on Saturday.

What a detection is. One detection is one satellite pixel, about 375 m across, seen radiating enough heat to be flagged as actively burning during a single overpass. A large fire front registers as many pixels at once, and a fire that keeps burning is counted again on every twice-daily pass it stays hot, so a weekly count measures how much ground was visibly alight and for how long. It is not a count of separate fires, and it is not burned area.

Method. NASA FIRMS active fire data from the VIIRS instrument on the Suomi-NPP satellite, used alone so that every year in the chart is measured by the same sensor. Low-confidence detections are excluded, and detections are assigned to countries using national boundary polygons applied identically in all years. The 2026 figures come from the near-real-time product; 2012 to 2025 come from the science-quality archive, which usually revises near-real-time counts slightly when it catches up. Heavy smoke and cloud suppress detections, so the worst fire days can be undercounted.

Attribution. Canadian boreal fire is driven by lightning, heat and drought in the northern forest, and its link to El Nino is weak. Canada is outside this tracker's El Nino regions and is shown for context only; nothing here presents Canadian fires as an El Nino signal.
