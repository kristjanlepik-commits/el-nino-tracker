What this is, and what it is not.

One question, asked of whatever is in the news: how big is this, actually? The limits come first, because they are what makes the rest believable.

01

The question

Not what caused it, and not what happens next. How big, measured against a computable historical baseline, with the sources named and dated.

Three things are distinguished throughout, and the site never collapses them:

The swell
Climate change. Decades of accumulated energy, the ground everything else happens on.
The wave
The 2026-27 El Niño. One large wave riding the swell, this season.
The break
The events reaching the news now. A fire week, a flood, a harvest.

The swell raised the ground the break happened on. That is a different claim from saying the wave caused it, and this site only makes the first.

02

What we do not do

No rival forecast
The headline probabilities are agency and model-ensemble outputs, recombined by arithmetic we publish. We do not issue a competing forecast of our own.
No causal attribution
We report whether an event falls inside a window where El Niño shifts the odds. Formal attribution is a separate scientific exercise and we defer to it.
No price forecasts
Where a physical quantity reaches a market we state the quantity and cite named analysis. We do not originate the number, and we make no trade recommendations.
No averaging away disagreement
When forecast centers disagree, the disagreement is the finding, and it is shown.
03

Where the numbers come from

Every input is a public agency or model output, fetched directly and carrying the date its publisher issued it, which is kept distinct from the date we retrieved it. An agency that has gone quiet reads as quiet rather than as current.

Where a published source exists, we use it. Where none exists and the question still matters, we build the measure ourselves, publish the working, and label the result as ours so it is never mistaken for an agency number.

The full source list and the arithmetic are on the methodology page.

04

The methodology is versioned

Every issue records the methodology version that produced it. When the arithmetic changes the version changes with it, and week-over-week comparisons that straddle a change are suppressed rather than quietly shown.

The current version is always shown on the methodology page.

05

The archive is immutable

Once an issue publishes it is frozen. Numbers are not revised in place, prose is not tidied, and the design it shipped with stays with it. Improvements apply to later issues only.

Every issue is in the archive, including the ones a later week proved wrong.

06

Attribution is stated, never implied

Every event carries one of exactly three statuses, and it is visible on the item rather than buried in a footnote. A site built around El Niño trains readers to assume it is behind everything, so each item has to say what it is not.

ENSO-loaded window a season and place where El Niño shifts the odds

Not ENSO-linked no established pathway ties this kind of event to El Niño

Attribution pending the formal attribution work is not in yet

07

The channels

Each channel reads one domain against its own baselines, as its own publication. A channel ships only once it has a baseline it can be measured against, which is why the list is short.

El Niño 2026-27, a weekly probability brief. Fires, hotspot activity against same-week satellite baselines, first issue 3 August 2026. Floods and a cross-channel damage ledger are planned and not yet published.

08

Who writes this

The Long Swell is an independent publication, written and edited by Kristjan Lepik. The methodology is shared with working climate scientists for external review. Contact details are in the footer.

09

Corrections

Past issues are never edited. A genuine rendering error on the current issue is fixed the day it is found, in a public commit; everything older is frozen for good.

This follows from the archive rule in section 05: an archive that can be edited is not a record.
