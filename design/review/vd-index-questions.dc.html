The map as a component, and a list problem for whenever the page comes

Scope, from Kristjan just now: the map is a COMPONENT to build on its own, and the full index page comes later. So the two questions below are about the map as a standalone element, not about a page around it. That helps: the second question, how to list and order cities, is a page problem rather than a map problem, and it can wait for the page.

This is what design has built. It is accurate and I am not confident it is right.

Filled: 2026 is that city's most hot days on record. Ring: elevated but not a record. Every city is in the warmest tenth of its own history, so a ring never means ordinary. Nothing between the marks is shaded, because we measured 21 stations and not a region.

1
Does it read as Europe, and does it need to?
The constraint was that it must not read as a temperature surface: we have no gridded product, so colouring a landmass would claim we measured one. I satisfied that by removing every geographic reference, and I think I may have overshot. A reader who does not already know where Zaragoza sits gets a scatter of labelled dots. Is a faint coastline the fix, or do the labels carry it, or is the arrangement itself enough? I have been wrong about this class of judgement three times today, so I would rather ask than decide.
2
Kristjan wants a list of cities on the index, at least a top ten. Ordering it is the problem.
His reasoning is that the map alone gives no way to scan, and it gets worse as cities are added. Agreed. But every ordering we could use is either invalid or degenerate.

By NIGHTS or DAYS COUNT: invalid. Each city has its own threshold, so Seville's 41.2 C and Berlin's 31.4 C are different questions. Ranking the counts against each other is the cross-city comparison the whole method refuses.

By PERCENTILE within own record: valid, and degenerate. 14 of the 21 sit at exactly 100.0, so a top ten is an arbitrary slice of a 14-way tie.

By RATIO to its own 1961-1990 baseline: comparable, and it flatters cities that used to have almost none. Marseille tops it at 23.7x, and three cities have baselines too thin to divide by at all.

By RANK: rank 1 of 106 in Madrid is a harder thing than rank 1 of 43 in Murcia, so the list would order by record length as much as by heat.
Where I would start, if it helps

The tie may be the finding rather than the obstacle. Fourteen cities at their own record is a stronger statement than any ranking of them, and a list that says so, alphabetically, asserts nothing false. The seven that are not are then a second, shorter list, and the reader learns the shape without anyone pretending Marseille beat Paris.

That also survives the set growing, which is the thing Kristjan is actually worried about. But it gives up the scan-and-compare that a ranked list provides, and if a reader wants to know whether their city is worse than the next one along, we are refusing to tell them. That refusal is either the product or a failure of it, and I do not think design should be the one to decide which.
